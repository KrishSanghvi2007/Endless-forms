I went through the structure and a representative spread of the implemented chapters. There is a lot worth preserving here. Endless Forms already behaves more like an interactive evolutionary laboratory than a conventional educational game. The strongest parts are where the player causes the phenomenon and then gets shown what happened.

The overall curriculum is also unusually ambitious: heredity → chance → selection → evolutionary games → sex → social evolution → behaviour → coevolution → genomes → speciation → ecology/deep time. Endless_Forms_v1.html

My biggest recommendation is not to add more content yet. Improve the feedback/learning loop and tighten a handful of scientific claims first.

1. Make every chapter follow: Predict → Act → Observe → Explain → Transfer

At present, many chapters essentially do:

instructions → play → result → explanation

I’d change the core loop to:

Prediction → Experiment → Result → “Why?” → new situation

That small change would dramatically improve learning.

For example, before a generation in The Lichen Wood:

Prediction: Which moths do you think will become more common?
Darker / Paler / No consistent change

Then let them hunt.

After breeding:

You predicted paler.
The survivors were 0.08 paler than the population.
Their offspring were 0.05 paler.

Your behaviour created selection.

Then, crucially:

What if the bark suddenly became dark?

Now they have to transfer the principle rather than merely observe it.

Your current game already calculates selection differential S, response R, and even realised heritability, which is excellent raw material for this. Endless_Forms_v1.html

⸻

2. Separate three kinds of truth

This is the most important accuracy improvement.

Every Notes screen should distinguish:

In this model

Exactly what the simulation assumes.

Biological principle

What the simplified model is designed to demonstrate.

In nature

What actual evidence shows—and where reality is messier.

Right now these sometimes blur together.

For example, your genetics engine deliberately models diploid additive traits with free recombination for many simulations. That’s perfectly reasonable pedagogically, but learners shouldn’t accidentally conclude that real quantitative traits generally consist of interchangeable additive loci with these mutation/effect distributions. Endless_Forms_v1.html

Put a tiny “Model assumptions” disclosure in Notes.

Something like:

This model simplifies reality. Beak size is represented by 10 additive loci plus environmental variation. Real beak morphology involves many interacting developmental and genetic effects.

That actually increases the intellectual credibility of the game without making gameplay harder.

⸻

3. One scientific correction I would make immediately: The Scribe

The chapter concept is excellent. Having the player physically copy DNA and then see silent, missense, nonsense and frameshift consequences is memorable.

But this sentence is too strong:

“A mutation is just a copying mistake.”

Mutations are not only replication errors. DNA sequence changes can arise through replication errors, DNA damage and repair processes, recombination-associated processes, transposable elements, etc.

Change it to approximately:

A mutation is a change in DNA sequence. Replication mistakes are one way mutations arise.

There is also a subtle problem with the human mutation-rate explanation. The historical Kong et al. result of roughly two additional mutations per year of paternal age is correctly represented as a result of that study, but later work found maternal-age effects too and estimates vary among datasets. 

So instead of presenting the Kong estimate as a timeless general constant, write:

Large sequencing studies find that children typically carry dozens of new germline mutations. The number rises with parental age, especially paternal age; the exact rate varies among studies and families.

Then optionally give Kong’s result as a historical example.

The underlying educational mechanic is strong enough that this correction won’t hurt it.

⸻

4. Mendel’s Garden is one of your strongest chapters

This is particularly good learning design.

The player isn’t being asked:

“What is a recessive allele?”

They’re given an inference problem:

Which purple plants secretly carry white?

And the optimal solution naturally leads them to discover why a test cross is useful.

That’s excellent.

Your probability treatment—ten offspring from a carrier × recessive test cross having a 1/2^{10} chance of all being purple—is also a nice way of teaching that genetic inference is probabilistic rather than magical. Endless_Forms_v1.html

I’d improve it with one feature:

Make confidence explicit.

Instead of only:

Pure / Carrier

let the player optionally say:

Pure — 99% confident

After ten purple offspring from a test cross:

If this plant were a carrier, this result would occur only about 1 time in 1,024.

This quietly teaches evidence, uncertainty and statistical inference alongside Mendelian genetics.

That’s much richer than simply teaching Punnett squares.

⸻

5. Don’t reward speed everywhere

Stars currently frequently reward reaching a target by an early generation.

That works as game design, but it can produce an unintended lesson:

faster evolution = better evolution

Evolution has no universal objective and doesn’t “win” by moving rapidly toward some endpoint.

Keep stars, but diversify what they mean.

For example:

★ Discover — achieve the phenomenon
★ Explain — correctly explain what caused it
★ Predict — predict a new condition correctly

Now mastery represents scientific understanding rather than optimisation alone.

For some chapters, efficiency absolutely makes sense. But it shouldn’t be the universal grammar of achievement.

⸻

6. Your best graphs should appear during play, not behind Notes

You already have a surprisingly capable visualization system: line charts, histograms, stacked bars, reference lines, distributions, environmental overlays, etc. Endless_Forms_v1.html

But the user has to choose Notes → Graphs.

That makes the scientific representation optional.

I’d occasionally transform the game world directly into the graph.

Example after a Sieve winter:

birds physically slide into a histogram → survivors remain coloured → offspring distribution fades in

Then label:

Before selection
Survivors
Offspring

That single animation could teach quantitative genetics better than several paragraphs.

The same principle works beautifully for:

* allele-frequency changes,
* drift,
* selection,
* bottlenecks,
* recombination,
* coalescent trees,
* frequency-dependent selection.

Make the graph feel like another view of the organisms, rather than a separate maths screen.

7. Drift Casino has an excellent premise; remove some gambling dominance

The conceptual core is strong:

random sampling can produce directional-looking changes even without selection.

And your explanation correctly highlights two important ideas: fixation probability depends on starting frequency and drift proceeds faster in smaller populations. Endless_Forms_v1.html

But chips/stakes/winning risks making learners optimise the betting system rather than reason about populations.

I’d retain the casino aesthetic but require a scientific prediction before betting:

Jar: N = 10, red = 80%

Which statement do you believe?

A. Red has an 80% chance of eventually fixing
B. Both colours have a 50% chance
C. Red must eventually fix

Then:

Place your bet.

Now the gambling metaphor reinforces probabilistic reasoning instead of competing with it.

⸻

8. The Sieve is exceptionally promising

Letting players literally draw the fitness/survival function is exactly the sort of interaction this project should pursue.

The three missions—move the mean, narrow the distribution, widen it—map naturally onto directional, stabilising and disruptive selection. Endless_Forms_v1.html

I’d add a fourth, secret challenge:

Can you make two distinct populations?

Let players try disruptive selection repeatedly.

They will probably discover that merely killing intermediates doesn’t necessarily produce two stable peaks because recombination/random mating regenerate intermediates.

Then reveal:

Disruptive selection isn’t automatically speciation.

That becomes a wonderful bridge into your later sympatric-speciation chapter.

It turns a failed objective into a conceptual discovery.

9. Be cautious with the fox domestication story

The Fancier has a great gameplay premise: select one characteristic and watch others respond. Endless_Forms_v1.html

But I’d soften the implication that the neural-crest explanation is established fact.

Instead of:

“One explanation is that genes affecting tameness also act on neural crest cells…”

I’d say something closer to:

Researchers have proposed several explanations for correlated domestication traits, including developmental mechanisms involving neural-crest cells. The causes remain debated.

More importantly, make the simulation explicit:

In this model, tameness is genetically correlated with ear and coat traits.

Otherwise the game risks presenting a debated biological explanation as something the simulation has demonstrated.

10. Industrial melanism: distinguish history from your counterfactual game

The carbonaria material is broadly grounded: the causal mutation was identified as a transposable-element insertion affecting cortex, with an inferred origin around 1819. 

But your gameplay compresses history dramatically and makes environmental change contingent on player progress—for example, the Clean Air Act can be triggered after black moths reach the game’s threshold. That’s good game pacing, but historically artificial. Endless_Forms_v1.html

Just tell the player:

History compressed: The game condenses more than a century of environmental change into a few generations. Your performance controls the timing for gameplay; historically, clean-air legislation obviously did not wait for moth frequencies.

That one sentence protects the historical lesson.

11. Batesian mimicry needs frequency dependence inside the mechanic

Your prose actually says something sophisticated:

mimics work while sufficiently rare because too many harmless mimics undermine the warning signal.

That’s important.

But the main objective is simply increasing resemblance to 70%. Endless_Forms_v1.html

I’d make the final stage expose the frequency dependence.

Once mimicry becomes common:

Predators begin testing red-and-black prey again.

Now the player’s behaviour changes because the signal has become less reliable.

That would turn the final explanatory paragraph into something the player experiences.

This is exactly the kind of transformation I’d pursue throughout the project:

Don’t tell the player the evolutionary principle if you can make the player become the selective environment that generates it.

12. Introduce “surprise me” experiments

After completing a chapter, unlock Sandbox.

Change one or two parameters:

* population size
* mutation rate
* heritability
* environmental variance
* migration
* recombination rate
* selection strength
* dominance

Then ask:

What do you think will happen?

Run it.

This creates genuine experimentation rather than puzzle solving.

It also solves an educational weakness of fixed goals: players learn that biological outcomes depend on parameters rather than memorising the scripted outcome.

13. Add counterfactual buttons to end screens

Your end screens currently provide an insight, chart, “real thing,” numbers, replay, and next chapter. That’s already a good architecture. Endless_Forms_v1.html

Add one more:

“What if…?”

Examples:

What if the population were 10× larger?

What if camouflage weren’t heritable?

What if carbonaria were recessive?

What if bitterwings disappeared?

What if crossovers happened twice as often?

Let the learner choose one and immediately rerun an accelerated simulation.

This is probably the single best feature you could add for causal understanding.

⸻

14. Make failure scientifically interesting

Avoid:

You failed. Try again.

Prefer:

Interesting result.

Then explain why.

For example:

You selected strongly, but the population barely changed. Why?

Possible answers:

* the trait wasn’t very heritable,
* selection was inconsistent,
* drift overwhelmed the response,
* gene flow opposed selection.

Failure becomes data.

That is much closer to real science.

⸻

15. Build explicit conceptual connections between chapters

Your curriculum is broad, but right now the notebook mostly presents chapters as individual destinations.

Make earlier discoveries reappear.

For example:

Mendel → Crossing Over

Mendel’s factors don’t always assort independently. Why?

Drift Casino → Castaways

You’ve seen what small N does. Now strand five founders on an island.

Lichen Wood → Sieve

Previously you were the selection pressure. Now draw the selection pressure directly.

Sieve → Speciation

You widened one population. Can selection actually split it?

Mutation → Selection

Mutation creates variation. Selection changes which variants reproduce.

Then the 40+ chapters become one argument about evolution, rather than a collection of excellent mini-games.

16. Reduce front-loaded reading

Your intro structure is sensible—hook, how to play, goal—and the UI explicitly provides Notes for deeper material. Endless_Forms_v1.html

I’d go further.

Aim for one sentence + one action before the player touches the system.

Instead of explaining three mechanics:

Something is hiding in these plants. Find it.

Then teach crossing by interaction.

After their first cross:

Ten offspring. Their colours tell you something about the parents.

Only introduce vocabulary after the learner has encountered the phenomenon.

Experience → name → formalise generally feels better here than definition → exercise.

17. Give every chapter one unforgettable sentence

After completion, show a one-line conceptual takeaway.

Examples:

Mendel

Recessive alleles can hide in plain sight.

Mutation

Mutation creates possibilities; it doesn’t create what organisms need.

Drift

Chance has evolutionary consequences when populations are finite.

Natural selection

Selection happens because different variants leave different numbers of descendants.

Recombination

Chromosomes are inherited in chunks, not gene by gene.

Hardy–Weinberg

Equilibrium is useful because departures tell you what to investigate.

Coalescent

Genealogies become simpler when evolution is run backwards.

These become retrieval cues later.

18. Add delayed retrieval

After three or four chapters, unexpectedly ask one question from an earlier chapter.

Not:

What is genetic drift?

Instead:

Two neutral alleles start at 50:50. Which population will usually reach fixation sooner: N=20 or N=2,000?

Later:

A rare neutral allele starts at 10%. Roughly what is its probability of eventually fixing?

This is far better for retention than awarding a star and never revisiting the concept.

20. The overarching design principle

I would use one rule when deciding whether to add anything:

Every important scientific idea should ideally be something the player causes, predicts, discovers, or fails to cause before the game names it.

Your best chapters already do this.

Mendel’s Garden makes dominance an inference problem. Crossing Over makes linkage spatial. The Lichen Wood turns the player into selection. Drift Casino makes randomness visible. The Sieve lets the learner literally draw selection.

That’s the distinctive identity of Endless Forms.

I would therefore spend the next development pass deepening those interactions rather than expanding the chapter count: add prediction, counterfactuals, model assumptions, uncertainty, retrieval, and transfer. The result would be more accurate and more enjoyable, because the educational improvement comes from giving the player more agency rather than giving them more text.
